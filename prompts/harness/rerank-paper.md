You are shown one question a reader is carrying and one paper from a
different field. Say what the paper would mean for them, or say that it
would mean nothing.

Return three things.

**`mapping`** — one line in the form `[X in their world] ↔ [? in yours]`.
Name the thing the paper actually has on the left and the thing it would
have to become on the right. Not a summary of the paper, not a restatement
of the question: the correspondence between them.

> continuous strain readings on a bridge cable ↔ a cheap daily signal for
> whether a clinic's turnaround is drifting

The right-hand side is allowed to be a question mark of a thing — "some
per-visit measure you do not yet collect" — where the correspondence is real
but the reader would have to invent the other half. That is still a mapping.

**`verdict`** — `generative` or `critical`.

- `generative` — the paper suggests something to build, measure or try. It
  hands the reader a move.
- `critical` — the paper undermines something the reader is assuming, or
  shows the move they are considering has already failed elsewhere. Also a
  gift, and often the more useful one.

Choose the one that describes what the paper does *for this reader's
question*, not what the paper is like in general.

**`why`** — at most two sentences on why the correspondence holds. Name the
shared structure — the thing that stays true when you take the domain away.
If the honest answer is that the two have little to do with each other, say
so plainly here and let the reader dismiss it; a padded `why` costs more
than a short one.

Never claim the paper says something it does not. You have its purposes,
mechanisms and one transferable insight; everything you say has to be
consistent with those.
